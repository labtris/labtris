title: "Deferred: wire routing"
description: "Why generated fabrics look tangled, what the graph-drawing literature says to do about it, and the order to do it in."

A generated spine-leaf reads worse than it should. Wires cross where
they do not need to, swing wide of the cards they connect, and pass
straight over node boxes on the way. None of that is a bug in the
sense of something being wrong — every wire connects the right two
ports. It is that the routing makes three decisions greedily and
locally, and none of the three can see the other two.

## What the renderer does today

All of it lives in `web/src/App.svelte`:

- **`portPos`** assigns the anchor slot by **interface index** —
  `y = p.y + 28 + (index * (H - 40)) / n`. eth0 always takes the top
  slot whether or not its peer is above or below. If eth0's peer sits
  below-right and eth1's above-right, those two wires cross each
  other immediately, at the card.
- **`portPos`** also picks the exit side one link at a time —
  `left = towards < p.x + W / 2` — with no view of the node's other
  links, so a node's wires can fan to opposite sides where grouping
  them would read better.
- **`wirePath`** draws a fixed-shape cubic with
  `reach = clamp(48, 200, dx * 0.5 + dy * 0.25)` and no awareness of
  obstacles. A mostly-vertical link still gets a large reach, which
  is where the wide arcs come from, and nothing keeps a wire off a
  card it happens to pass over.

The first of those is responsible for most of the visible mess, and
is also the cheapest to fix.

## What the literature says

Layered graph drawing is a solved-enough problem and a spine-leaf,
rail-optimised or fat-tree fabric is exactly a layered graph.

- **Sugiyama (1981)** is the canonical framework and what Graphviz
  `dot` implements: cycle removal, layer assignment, **crossing
  reduction**, coordinate assignment. `topology_gen.py` already does
  layer assignment — spines row 0, leaves row 1, hosts row 2 — and
  then skips crossing reduction entirely.
- **Brandes–Köpf (2002)** is the standard coordinate-assignment pass:
  linear time, straight vertical runs, balanced result.
- Exact crossing minimisation is NP-hard, so the practical move is
  the per-layer median/barycenter heuristic. The parallel with
  Knuth–Plass is real — replace greedy local choices with a global
  objective, then accept a tractable approximation of it.
- **Tamassia's Topology-Shape-Metrics (1987)** covers orthogonal
  right-angle routing, the conventional network-diagram look.
  `libavoid` (Adaptagrams, used by Inkscape and Dia) is the
  battle-tested implementation of obstacle-avoiding connectors.
- **Holten (2006)** and **Holten & van Wijk (2009)** on edge bundling
  are what make a dense Clos legible at all — bundles read as cable
  trunks rather than 32 individual wires.
- **Force-directed** layouts (Fruchterman–Reingold, Kamada–Kawai) are
  the wrong tool here. They suit organic graphs and would wobble a
  structured fabric into a blob.
- **elkjs** (JS port of the Eclipse Layout Kernel) is the most
  directly usable library: Sugiyama plus Brandes–Köpf plus orthogonal
  edge routing, and — the part that matters — **port constraints**,
  so ports can be pinned to a side and ordered. dagre is the older JS
  Sugiyama implementation and is effectively unmaintained.

## The order to do it in

**1. Order anchor slots by peer position, not interface index.**
Leave the curve alone; sort each node's links by the peer's `y` (or
by angle) and hand out slots in that order. Kills the crossings that
happen at the card, which is most of what is visible. Roughly 30
lines in `portPos`, no new dependency. This is the one worth doing
first by a wide margin.

**2. Reorder within each layer** with the barycenter heuristic before
`topology_gen.py` writes geometry. Sugiyama's missing phase 3, and
close to free because the generator already owns the coordinates.

**3. Obstacle-aware or orthogonal routing** via elkjs or libavoid.
A real project, and the point at which the canvas stops being
hand-rolled SVG. Worth it for the conventional right-angle look.

**4. Edge bundling**, once fabrics get dense enough to need it — a
k=4 fat-tree with 16 hosts is about where individual wires stop
being readable.

## The caveat that interacts with all of it

The anchors do not sit on the `eth` chips. `.ports` is a wrapping
flex row at the bottom of the card, not a column down its edge, so
there is no per-port position on the border to anchor to. Even
perfect routing will not make a wire visually terminate at the port
it represents. Steps 1 and 2 are still worth doing as they stand,
but "wires emerge from the chips" is a change to the card's own
layout and should be decided on purpose rather than fallen into.

Related: `H = 92` in `App.svelte` is a spread parameter, not a
measured height — the card actually renders 98px tall. It only
matters if the anchors ever become real measurements.
